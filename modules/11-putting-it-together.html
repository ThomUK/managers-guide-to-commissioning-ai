Putting it together

You need this if you want to turn the course into questions for a real decision.

The key idea

Every choice in this course is a trade between capability, speed, cost and control. You do not need to make the technical trade-offs yourself. You need to know they exist and what to ask.

Four things being traded

Capability is how good the answers are: larger models are generally better. Speed is how long people wait. Cost is what you pay, whether per seat, per token, per hour or up front. Control is where your data goes and who can change what the system does.

You rarely get more of one without giving up some of another. A bigger model is more capable, and slower and dearer to run. Running it yourself gives control, and takes on cost and effort. An agent that acts without asking is faster, and gives up some control.

Each part of this course looked at some of these trades. Part A was about using AI: what a model is doing (module 1), who owns what (module 2), what an agent may touch (modules 3 and 4) and the cheapest route to better results (module 5). Part B was about running it: whether a model fits (module 6), why it is fast or slow (modules 7 and 8) and the two ways of getting more from one machine (modules 9 and 10). The explorer above puts Part B in one place. Try a model that does not fit, then lower its precision.

Do we need to run it ourselves at all?

This is the first question, and for most organisations and most uses the answer is no. The path below starts there.

Questions to ask

About any AI product

  • Who owns the model? Who owns the GPU? How do we pay? Where does our data go?

About an agent

  • What can it read? What can it change? What needs approval? What is logged?

About results

  • Have we tried prompting and RAG before anything more expensive? How will we know it improved?

About hardware

  • Which model, at what precision, for how many people at once, at what speed, drawing how much power, hosted where?

The detail your technical team handles optional

How modules 6 to 10 connect

A model is a file of numbers that must fit in fast memory, with room to spare (module 6). A response is made in two phases: the prompt is read in one go, then the answer is written one token at a time, with the KV cache holding the model’s notes (module 7). Writing speed is set by memory bandwidth, because each token needs the weights to flow from memory to the processor once (module 8). There are then two ways to get more from the same machine. A mixture-of-experts model reads only part of itself for each token, which helps speed but not fit (module 9). Quantisation stores every number less precisely, which helps both fit and speed at some cost in quality (module 10). The sizing explorer above is those five ideas in one place.
